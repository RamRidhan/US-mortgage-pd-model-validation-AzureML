# Decisions log

Format: Decision | Alternative | Why

## Step 1: Load data and define default

**D1. Default definition.** A loan-month is a default event if delinquency status is 3+ (90+ days past due), or `RA` (REO acquisition), or zero-balance code is 02 (third-party sale), 03 (short sale / charge-off), 09 (REO disposition) or 15 (whole-loan / note sale). `default_12m = 1` if any default event occurs in the 12 months after the snapshot.
- Alternative: 60+ DPD, or 90+ only without credit-event terminations.
- Why: mirrors the Basel/CRR 90-day backstop used by UK IRB models; the termination codes catch loans that leave the file in a credit event without a recorded 90+ status. ZB 01 (prepaid), 16 (reperforming securitisation) and 96 (defect repurchase, before a credit event) are not defaults.

**D2. 12-month horizon.** Alternative: 6 or 24 months. Why: matches the one-year PD horizon of IRB models, and gives a reasonable event count at roughly 0.4% to 0.6% annual default rates in 2015-2018.

**D3. Exclude loans already in default at the snapshot** (plus loans already terminated). Alternative: include them with a flag. Why: the model is a PD for performing loans. Defaulted loans have PD = 1 by definition under the CRR, and including them would inflate Gini through trivial separation and double count defaults across snapshots.

**D4. Snapshots** at each December, 2015 to 2019, pooled. Alternative: a single snapshot, or monthly. Why: gives several economic conditions for the later out-of-time and stability tests, without heavily overlapping observations of the same loans.

**D5. Layout deviations from the guide snippet (checked against the General User Guide Release 47 and the Disclosure Changes summary).**
- The sample origination files have 31 fields, not 32: Servicer Name is not present. `servicer` was removed from `ORIG_COLS`; the `assert` on column count caught this.
- Performance files are named `sample_perf_*.txt`, not `sample_svcg_*`.
- Performance files have 35 fields and still hold Defect Settlement Date at position 7, so the 0-based positions 0-4, 7, 8, 9 are correct.

**D6. Missing/unknown delinquency (`XX`) at the snapshot:** loan dropped, since `cur_dq` is undefined. Loans that terminate in the window without a default event (e.g. prepayment) are treated as `default_12m = 0` (non-default, not censored).

**D7. Feature timing.** `cur_dq`, `max_dq_12m` (12 months to and including the snapshot), `months_since_dq` (full history to the snapshot, 999 if never delinquent), `ever_modified`, `upb_ratio` and `loan_age` use data at or before the snapshot only.

## Step 2: Development, out-of-time and monitoring samples

**D8. Time-based samples.** Development = Dec 2015, 2016, 2017 snapshots (outcomes 2016-2018). OOT = Dec 2018 (outcome 2019). Monitoring = Dec 2019 (outcome 2020, COVID). Alternative: random split. Why: the model is applied to future loans, so the honest test is a later period; a random split would let the model see the same macro conditions it is tested on. The COVID snapshot is deliberately kept out of development so it stresses the model (drift, PSI, actual vs expected) and supports a post model adjustment finding. This replaces my earlier suggestion in Step 1 to use 2019 as OOT.

**D9. 70/30 split by loan, not by row.** `GroupShuffleSplit(train_size=0.7, random_state=42)` on `loan_id`. Alternative: split by row. Why: a loan appears in up to three development snapshots; a row split would put the same loan in train and holdout and inflate holdout performance. Disjointness is asserted in code.

**D10. OOT is not purged of development loans.** 80.7% of Dec 2018 loans also appear in the development sample (same loan, later snapshot). Alternative: drop them from OOT. Why kept: dropping them would leave only 2018-originated and newly seasoned loans, an unrepresentative OOT. The OOT is still honest in time (outcome window is after every development outcome window), but it is not independent by borrower; to be raised as a limitation in the validation report.

**D11. Interest-only and ARM loans.** None are present in this Standard Dataset sample (`io_flag` all N, `amort_type` all FRM), so the exclusions remove 0 loans; the filters stay in the code so a new data release fails visibly. 

**D12. HARP refinances: kept with the `harp` flag, not excluded.** Alternative: exclude. Why: 16% of loan-snapshots (22% in Dec 2015) are HARP, so excluding them would make the model unrepresentative of the book. Their risk differs (high LTV relief refinances), so `harp` stays a candidate variable in Step 3 and the validation report should check performance with and without HARP.

**D13. Invalid key fields are not excluded in Step 2.** FICO 9999 (100 rows), DTI 999 (168,270 rows, 16% of the sample), LTV/CLTV 999 (24) and units 99 (148) are counted in results.md and handled in Step 3 (missing treated as its own WoE bin, not imputed, for DTI). 

## Step 3: Data-quality checks

**D14. Sentinels become missing, not imputed.** FICO 9999, LTV/CLTV 999, DTI 999, units 99 and first-time-buyer 9 are set to NaN; each gets its own WoE bin in Step 4. Alternative: median imputation. Why: "not available" is itself informative and imputation would invent values; a WoE missing bin lets the data decide its risk.

**D15. DTI missing is structural, not random.** 168,220 of the 168,270 DTI = 999 rows are HARP loans (HARP refinances do not report DTI; P(DTI missing | HARP) = 100%). Treatment: missing bin kept. Consequence: DTI missing and `harp` are almost the same variable, so they must not both enter the scorecard unchecked (collinearity); decide in Step 4/5 using IV and correlation.

**D16. LTV above 105 is kept.** All 36,053 such rows are HARP loans (relief refinances can have LTV above 105 by design); there are none outside HARP. Alternative: cap or set to missing. Why: they are valid values and WoE binning absorbs the tail. CLTV above 200 (1,753 rows) is outside the guide range and is set to missing.

**D17. UPB above original (431 rows).** Flagged (`upb_gt_orig`) and `upb_ratio` capped at 1; rows kept. Alternative: drop. Why: small, plausible (rounding of early balances, capitalisation after modification) and dropping performing loans would bias the sample.

**D18. Fields dropped.** `pre_harp_id` (84% blank, an identifier whose information is already in `harp`), and `ppm`, `amort_type`, `io_flag`, `mi_cancel` (constant in this sample). `program` is 98% blank but blank means "no special programme", so it is recoded to 'N' and kept as a category, not dropped. `msa` stays in the data but is not a candidate variable (about 400 levels, 13.7% blank; state used for geography).

**D19. No duplicates, no time gaps.** 0 duplicate loan_id + period rows in 23.8M rows and 0 loans with gaps in the 12 months before a snapshot, so no behavioural-feature treatment is needed.

**D20. Target sanity.** Snapshot default rates are 0.35% to 0.56% outside COVID and 3.99% in Dec 2019, moving with the economy, not randomly. By vintage (Dec 2015-2018 snapshots, excluding COVID) the rate is 0.37% to 0.55%. I first pooled vintages across all snapshots, which showed 0.77% to 2.50% rising with vintage; that was an artefact, since young vintages appear only in the later snapshots including COVID. Excluded the monitoring snapshot from that chart for that reason.

**D21. Cleaned samples are saved separately** as `data/interim/{train,holdout,oot,monitoring}_clean.parquet`; the originals are untouched. Treatments are defined once in `src/dq.py:clean_features`. They are rule-based (no statistic is fitted on any sample), so applying them to OOT and monitoring does not leak.

## Step 4: WoE binning and variable selection (train split only)

**D22. Binning set-up.** `optbinning.OptimalBinning`, max 8 bins, 4 bins requested where the data allow it, `monotonic_trend="auto_asc_desc"` for numerics, categorical bins grouped by event rate (`cat_cutoff=0.01`), missing in its own bin. Fitted on the train split only; other samples are only transformed.
- Deviation 1: min bin size is 5% (default for most variables) but 0.1% for `cur_dq`, `max_dq_12m`, `months_since_dq`, `ever_modified` and `units`, and their CART pre-binning leaf size is also 0.1%. Why: 2 months past due is 0.1% of train loans (528) with a 64% default rate; a 5% minimum bin returned a single bin and IV = 0 for the strongest behavioural variables, which would have been a silent error.
- Deviation 2: binary or near-binary variables (`ever_modified`, `n_borrowers`, `units`) and `cur_dq`/`orig_term`/`loan_age` end with 2-3 bins, fewer than the 4 requested, because the data have no more distinct risk levels.
- WoE sign is ln(%goods/%bads): positive WoE = safer.

**D23. Rule 1, Information Value.** Drop IV < 0.02; flag IV > 0.5 for a leakage check. Dropped: purpose (0.016), loan_age (0.013), occupancy (0.012), channel (0.009), first_time_buyer (0.007), prop_type (0.001), units (0.000). Flagged: months_since_dq (2.44), max_dq_12m (2.34), cur_dq (2.05), fico (1.00).
- Leakage check on the delinquency fields: (a) they are built only from periods up to and including the snapshot; (b) loans already 90+ days past due, in REO or terminated at the snapshot are excluded, so no loan carries its default into the target; (c) the strength is a roll-rate effect: a loan 2 payments behind has a 64% chance of reaching 90+ DPD in 12 months (1 payment behind: 17%; current: 0.28%), and the pattern holds in every development snapshot (IV 1.5 to 3.2 per snapshot, Spearman 0.90 to 1.00). So strong but legitimate, not leaked. Consequence: the champion model will be dominated by a few behavioural variables, and Gini will be high because most of its power comes from the 0.8% of loans that are already delinquent; the validation report should show Gini with and without the behavioural block.
- FICO at IV 1.00 is an origination variable and cannot leak. The IV is large because defaults are rare (0.46%): the default rate falls from 2.15% below FICO 662 to 0.09% above 790, a 23x gradient, monotone across all 8 bins.

**D24. Rule 2, direction.** Expected: risk falls with FICO and months_since_dq, rises with LTV, CLTV, DTI, rate, cur_dq, max_dq_12m and ever_modified. All variables with a stated expectation pass. Variables with no prior (n_borrowers, upb_ratio, orig_term): kept only where I can explain the sign. n_borrowers: two borrowers default less than one (0.28% vs 0.65%; two incomes). upb_ratio: loans that have paid down more are safer. orig_term: shorter terms are safer (faster amortisation, self-selected borrowers).

**D25. Rule 3, stability.** Require the bin default-rate ordering to hold in each of the Dec 2015, 2016 and 2017 snapshots (Spearman vs train-wide ordering >= 0.8). `state` fails (0.60), despite IV 0.24: the fitted state groups are non-contiguous (e.g. OR/TN/CO together), FL/TX are the only states with a clear story, and the ordering moves between snapshots. Dropped, and noted as a possible later extension with judgemental regional groups. Also noted: `purpose`, `channel`, `first_time_buyer` and `prop_type` would have failed stability as well.

**D26. Rule 4, correlation on WoE values, |r| > 0.7.** Keep the higher-IV variable. Dropped: `ltv` (r = 0.94 with `cltv`; cltv has the higher IV, 0.28 vs 0.23, and covers total leverage) and `max_dq_12m` (r = 0.89 with `months_since_dq`). `cur_dq` and `months_since_dq` correlate at 0.695, just below the cut-off, and both are kept: `cur_dq` says how far behind a loan is now, `months_since_dq` how recently it was behind. This is a judgement at the margin, to be re-tested in Step 5 (coefficient signs and significance).

**D27. Rule 5, VIF < 5.** Max VIF in the final set is 2.02 (months_since_dq, orig_term), so nothing is dropped. The 100,000-row random sample of the train WoE values was used for speed.

**D28. Final set: 10 variables.** months_since_dq, cur_dq, fico, orig_rate, dti, cltv, ever_modified, n_borrowers, upb_ratio, orig_term. 
- `harp` was not in the candidate list, so it is not in the model, but the DTI missing bin is 99.97% HARP loans (D15), so the model sees HARP through that bin (missing-DTI default rate is 0.93% against 0.46% overall).
- `ever_modified` rests on only 473 train loans (0.1%), of which 21.6% default; it is stable across snapshots (IV 0.21 to 0.23) but thin, so the validation report should treat it as a low-confidence variable.

## Step 5: Champion scorecard

**D29. Sign convention.** The guide says optbinning's WoE has the opposite sign to the Step 4 formula. I checked: it does not. optbinning's WoE is ln(%goods/%bads) (low-FICO bins have negative WoE and a high default rate), the same as the Step 4 formula, so a higher WoE means safer. Convention used: tables and the points table show WoE = ln(%goods/%bads); the logistic regression is fitted on risk-WoE = -WoE so that higher means riskier and every coefficient must be positive. Alternative: fit on WoE and expect negative coefficients. Why: positive coefficients are easier to read and to test for wrong signs.

**D30. Sign check removed `orig_term`.** In the first fit `orig_term` had a negative coefficient (-0.23), although its univariate trend is correct (shorter term = safer). It is correlated with `orig_rate` (0.59) and `upb_ratio` (0.58) on WoE. Per the rule, removed and refit; all nine remaining coefficients are positive with p < 0.001. The final model has 9 variables (D28 had 10).

**D31. Scorecard scaling.** 600 points at 50:1 good:bad odds, 20 points to double the odds (Factor = 28.85, Offset = 487.12). The intercept is spread equally across variables, so each variable's points are Offset/n + Factor x (-b0/n + b_i x WoE_bin). The points table was checked to reproduce the model score for 2,000 loans to 1e-13. Step 7 shifts the constant after calibration.

**D32. Overfitting check.** Gini is 0.803 on train and 0.784 on the in-time holdout, a gap of 0.019 (holdout is split by loan, D9). That is small, so no further regularisation.

## Step 6: Challenger and decision (made on train/holdout only, before the OOT sample is opened)

**D33. Challenger set-up.** LightGBM with the guide's parameters, on raw variables (14 numeric candidates plus 5 categoricals, `state` excluded as in D25), monotone constraints in the Step 4 direction. Alternative: same 9 variables as the scorecard. Why: the challenger exists to show whether the scorecard leaves signal behind, so it sees every candidate. No hyperparameter tuning was done; a tuned GBM might gain a little.

**D34. Decision: the scorecard stays champion; the GBM is kept as a benchmark.** In-time holdout Gini is 0.7837 for the scorecard and 0.7803 for the GBM, so the challenger does not win even on accuracy (-0.0034). It also overfits more (train-to-holdout gap 0.083 vs 0.019) and its average predicted PD on holdout is 0.473% vs 0.445% observed, against a scorecard intercept fixed by construction. The scorecard has points per bin that a credit officer can read, simple monitoring (PSI per bin) and a clear governance route under SS1/23; the GBM needs SHAP, and its SHAP ranking (FICO, borrowers, DTI, UPB ratio, loan age) differs from the scorecard's, so the two do not even agree on what drives risk (the delinquency variables matter less to the GBM only because they cover 0.8% of loans). Condition for adopting a GBM later: a clear Gini gain on OOT (say 3+ points) that holds on the COVID sample, stable SHAP rankings across snapshots, and calibration by grade as good as the scorecard's. OOT and stability columns of the comparison are filled in after Step 7 and Step 8, as a check on this decision, not as an input to it.

## Step 7: Calibration, master scale, back-test (7a/7b decided on development data, before the OOT sample was scored)

**D35. Calibration.** Intercept shift of -0.0152 log-odds so the mean PD on the development sample equals its observed default rate (0.4542%, Dec 2015-2017 pooled). Alternative: Platt/isotonic recalibration, or a calibration slope as well as intercept. Why: an intercept shift keeps the ranking and the points structure, and is the standard anchoring step for a PD scorecard. Limitation to carry into the validation report: this anchor covers 2016-2018 only, a benign period without a downturn, so it is a point-in-time-benign estimate, not a through-the-cycle long-run average as IRB requires; PDs are likely too low for a downturn.

**D36. Master scale: 9 grades, edges at PD 0.035%, 0.05%, 0.1%, 0.2%, 0.4%, 0.8%, 1.6%, 4.0%.** Alternative: the guide's `geomspace(0.0003, 0.30)`. Why changed: that gives 11 groups and the top five hold only 0.2% to 1.1% of loans each (checked on development data only). The model's PD range is wide (0.01% to 88%) but 98% of loans sit below PD 1.6%, so a strict doubling scale cannot also keep every grade at about 1% of loans. Grades 3 to 8 double the PD of the previous grade; grade 1 and 2 are narrower (a 0.035% edge splits the safest 11% of loans); grade 9 (PD above 4%) is open-ended with mean PD 21%, 9x grade 8, because it is the population that is already delinquent. Every grade holds at least 1.0% of development loans and has defaults, but grade 1 has only 2 development defaults, so its PD is estimated from model scores, not from observed defaults. Edges are fixed from the development data and applied unchanged to OOT and monitoring.

**D37. Grade PD = mean calibrated model PD of the development loans in the grade** (as in the guide). Alternative: observed default rate per grade. Why: the model PD is smoother in sparse grades and the back-test then tests the model, not the grade averages.

**D38. Back-test method.** One-sided binomial test (and Jeffreys test alongside) of observed defaults against the grade PD, traffic light on the binomial p-value (Green >= 0.05, Amber 0.01 to 0.05, Red < 0.01). A lower-tail test flags grades that are too conservative (p < 0.05). Caveat: both tests assume independent defaults; real defaults are correlated through the macro cycle, so the tests reject more often than they should, and a Red here is a prompt to investigate, not proof. Multiple testing: 9 grades, so about 0.5 false alarms at the 5% level are expected by chance.

## Step 7: findings after scoring the OOT sample (Dec 2018, scored once; no design change made afterwards)

**D39. Discrimination holds.** OOT Gini 0.8430 vs in-time holdout 0.7837 (a gain, not a drop). This is not evidence the model improved: Gini on one snapshot varies a lot (development snapshots: 0.82, 0.76, 0.83; the pooled development sample is 0.80), and 80.7% of OOT loans are development loans seen a year later (D10). The OOT figure is inside the development range. On currently-performing loans only (cur_dq = 0) Gini is 0.72 OOT vs 0.68 pooled development, so the model discriminates well without the delinquency signal as well.

**D40. Overall calibration is slightly conservative.** OOT actual default rate 0.449% vs expected 0.482% (actual/expected 0.932), with the 2016-2018 anchor of 0.454%. Overall the PDs overstate 2019 risk by about 7%. Nothing to adjust at portfolio level.

**D41. Grade-level calibration is a structural slope problem, not a time effect.** Back-test (binomial): grade 8 Red (0.0000; observed 4.03% vs PD 2.38%, 1.7x), grade 7 Amber (0.024; 1.24x), grades 1, 5, 6 Green, grade 9 Green. Grades 3, 4, 5 and 9 are significantly too conservative (observed 0.49x, 0.49x, 0.57x of PD for 3 to 5; 0.92x for grade 9, p = 0.014); grade 2 is borderline (0.16x, p = 0.015, on 1 default). The same pattern is visible in-sample on development data (grades 3-4 observed 0.4x and 0.7x of PD; grades 6-8 observed 1.3x, 1.3x, 1.4x of PD), so it is the shape of the PD curve (too flat across grades 3 to 8), not 2019 behaviour. Remedy (not applied, to keep the OOT test clean): recalibrate with intercept plus slope on development data and re-test on a new sample. The consequence of leaving it: provisions and capital for the middle grades are understated (grades 6 to 8) while the safest grades are overstated. Low power in grades 1 and 2 (3 and 1 observed defaults).

**D42. Challenger on OOT (for the comparison table only, decision D34 unchanged).** GBM OOT Gini 0.8312 vs scorecard 0.8430, and its mean predicted PD is 0.533% vs 0.449% actual (over-predicts by 19%, scorecard 7%), so the OOT result supports keeping the scorecard as champion.

## Step 8: Monitoring MI pack (reports/MI_pack.pdf, 8 pages)

**D43. RAG thresholds are my choice**, taken from the brief (common industry rules of thumb, not regulatory values). Gini drop is relative to the pooled development Gini (0.7973). Back-test RAG for a snapshot is the worst grade light. CSI uses the PSI thresholds.

**D44. Result.** OOT Dec 2018: Green on PSI (grade 0.0018, score 0.0012), Gini (0.8430, drop -5.7%), actual/expected (0.932) and CSI (worst orig_rate 0.067); Red on the grade back-test only because grade 8 is Red (grade 7 Amber), the slope issue in D41. Dec 2019: PSI Green (0.0077 / 0.0064), but Gini 0.4759 (drop 40.3%, Red), all 9 grades Red on the back-test (observed 1.6x to 21x grade PD), actual/expected 7.53 (3.99% vs 0.53%, Red) and CSI Red (upb_ratio 0.49, because the sample contains no 2019 originations, so the book seasons and pays down; the other variables are under 0.07).

**D45. The Dec 2019 failure is a problem with the outcome, not with the model's inputs or ranking.** Evidence from the layout's forbearance fields (Borrower Assistance Plan, Payment Deferral Flag, Delinquency Due to Disaster): in Apr-Dec 2020, 86.1% of loan-months under a forbearance plan show 30+ DPD (49.7% show 90+) against 0.9% / 0.2% for loans with no flag, so forborne loans do show as delinquent. Of the 9,059 Dec 2019 'defaults', 8,466 (93.5%) were forborne at or before the default; 8,477 (93.6%) cured to current or paid off by March 2026; only 136 (1.5%) ended in a credit-event termination (REO, short sale, charge-off, note sale), 61 of them forborne. For Dec 2018 the equivalent figures are 209 forborne of 1,141 (18%) and 831 cured or paid off (73%), 160 credit events, so curing is normal for 90+ DPD mortgages and forbearance adds to it. Judgement: most 2020 'defaults' under the 90-day rule are payment holidays, not credit losses, so the 90-day definition overstates credit default in 2020. Treating the 8,029 loans that were forborne and later cured or paid off (no credit-event termination) as non-defaults, Dec 2019 gives: default rate 0.453%, actual/expected 0.857 (Green), Gini 0.8049 (Green, drop -0.9%), and the grade back-test shows 7 Green, 1 Amber, 1 Red, the same shape as OOT (grade 8 Red). Caveats: this uses outcome data to March 2026 (hindsight a monitoring team would not have had in 2020); loans that 'cured' with a payment deferral still carry deferred balances that may be lost later; the 61 + 75 credit-event loans are real losses and the model did not anticipate them as a group. The strong rank ordering after adjustment says the scorecard still identifies weak borrowers; it cannot see forbearance status, which was not a data field it used.

**D46. Recommendation for the validation report:** do not recalibrate on the reported 2020 default rate (it would multiply PDs by 7.5 for a payment holiday); keep the 90-day definition for IRB/regulatory consistency but carry a forbearance flag in monitoring; add forbearance status as a monitored data field and test a definition that counts forborne-then-cured loans separately.

## Step 9: IFRS 9 extension (illustrative; Dec 2019 portfolio of the sample, 227,165 loans, $42.3bn)

**D47. Staging rule (my design).** Stage 2 if 30+ DPD or PD >= 2.5x its PD when first observed and PD > 0.3%; otherwise Stage 1. "First observed" = the loan's earliest scored snapshot (Dec 2015 to Dec 2018); 5,946 loans with no earlier snapshot get a ratio of 1 (this is not origination PD: a behavioural model cannot score at origination, so loans first seen in 2015 are measured from 2015). Stage 3 is out of scope because loans already in default are excluded in Step 1. Result: Stage 1 219,026 loans, $40.70bn; Stage 2 8,139 loans, $1.57bn (3.7% of balance), of which 2,283 are 30+ DPD and 5,856 are on the relative-PD test only. Staging is set at Dec 2019 and held fixed across scenarios; a real engine would also restage under each scenario, so downside ECL is understated.

**D48. Lifetime PD.** Hazard by loan age = observed 12-month default rate of performing loan-months by loan-age year, using snapshots up to Dec 2018 only (outcome windows end before the Dec 2019 reporting date, so no hindsight, and no COVID). Observed ages stop at year 6 (sample vintages 2012-2018), so the hazard is held flat beyond that: a limitation for the 30-year horizon. I deviated from the brief's index by using age a+t-1 in year t so that year-1 hazard equals the loan's own 12-month PD. Each loan keeps its relative risk (PD / base hazard) for life, with no mean reversion: this inflates Stage 2 for delinquent loans (mean lifetime PD 47%) and means 52% of weighted ECL comes from the 1% of loans that are 30+ DPD. A reversion factor is the obvious refinement.

**D49. ECL set-up.** EAD = scheduled level-payment balance at the start of each year from current UPB, original rate and remaining term to maturity; no prepayment (overstates ECL; with fast US prepayment the overstatement can be large). Discount at the original rate. LGD 10% assumed for prime US mortgages (not estimated from the loss fields). Stage 1 = year 1 only; Stage 2 = full horizon, capped at 30 years or remaining term.

**D50. Unemployment link.** logit(monthly portfolio default rate) = alpha + beta x dU, 84 month-end snapshots Jan 2013 to Dec 2019, dU = change in FRED UNRATE over the same 12-month outcome window. beta = 0.1817 (OLS standard error 0.0244; Newey-West standard error 0.0623 because overlapping windows make OLS errors too small), R-squared 0.40. Pre-COVID only (2013-2018): beta = 0.3049 (OLS SE 0.0650, Newey-West 0.0947). The headline beta is the specified one and is probably biased by the forbearance-driven 2020 defaults (D45) and by hindsight on 2020 outcomes; I used 0.1817 for the headline and report 0.3049 as a sensitivity (weighted ECL $64.8m instead of $60.5m). The regression is weak evidence: the series also contains a hurricane effect (31% of Dec 2016 snapshot defaults had a disaster flag, 15% for Dec 2017, against 2.5% and 3.5% in Dec 2015 and Dec 2018), which corrects my remark in D20 that the target moves only with the economy: the Dec 2016 and 2017 snapshot rates (0.56%, 0.44%) include Harvey/Irma/Maria delinquencies, and this probably also explains why `state` was unstable in Step 4.

**D51. Scenarios (my assumption, labelled illustrative).** Upside -0.5 pp (20%), Base 0.0 pp (50%; the brief's base value was garbled, so I assumed zero), Downside +3.0 pp (30%).

**D52. Result.** ECL: Upside $53.14m, Base $55.69m, Downside $73.54m, weighted $60.54m (coverage 0.143%; base 0.132%). LGD sensitivity (weighted): 5% $30.27m, 10% $60.54m, 20% $121.07m (linear in LGD). Why weighted > base: of the $4.85m gap, $4.31m is the weights, not convexity: the scenarios are skewed to the downside (expected dU +0.8 pp, so even a linear model gives a higher weighted figure), and $0.54m is convexity (PD rises faster in logit space for higher dU and is capped at 1, but with beta 0.18 per pp the curve is not very convex). So "losses rise faster in a downturn than they fall in an upturn" is true here (downside +$17.9m vs upside -$2.5m for 3.0 pp vs 0.5 pp), but mostly because the downside shock is six times larger and 50% more likely (30% vs 20% weight) than the upside.

**D53. PMA note (half page).** *What it would do.* At Dec 2019 the model needs no COVID adjustment: it has no information about 2020. The question arises at the first 2020 reporting dates, when the model, or a recalibration triggered by the Red monitoring pack, reads forbearance-driven delinquency as default. If PDs were rescaled mechanically to the reported 2020 rate (actual/expected 7.53), the Dec 2019 ECL would rise from $55.7m to $166.0m (+198%). Because 93.5% of those defaults were forborne and 93.6% cured or paid off, scaling to the forbearance-adjusted experience (0.86) gives $51.0m (-8.4%). *Size.* A downward PMA of about $115m (-69% of the mechanical figure) would bring a mechanically recalibrated ECL back to the adjusted view; relative to the model as it stands the adjusted view is -$4.7m (-8.4%), which I would not release because the 0.86 ratio is within the calibration noise seen in OOT (0.93) and uses hindsight. A second, upward adjustment of +$1.3m (+2.4%) is justified at Dec 2019 by the grade-level slope problem in D41 (grades 7-8 under-predicted, grades 3-5 over-predicted), using OOT ratios available at the date. Net PMA at Dec 2019 therefore: +$1.3m (about +2%), at Dec 2020: hold the reported-default effect out and size it from forbearance cure evidence. *Release.* Review quarterly. Release the forbearance overlay in proportion to the share of forborne loans that cure or pay off (here 93.6% ex post), switching off when the forbearance flag falls below 1% of loan-months, and remove the grade-slope overlay once the model is recalibrated with intercept and slope (D41). Every PMA should be logged with owner, size, rationale and expiry date, as SS1/23 and IFRS 9 governance expect.

**D54. Limitations carried to the validation report:** benign 2016-2018 calibration anchor (D35); ages above 6 years unobserved; no prepayment; persistent relative risk; staging fixed across scenarios; LGD assumed; scenarios and weights illustrative; beta weak and influenced by hurricanes and forbearance; ECL is for a Freddie Mac sample (350,000 loans originated 2012-2018), not a whole book.
